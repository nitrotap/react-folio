1d:T33d1,<p>My permutation harness proves that a resampler is a bijection for <code>n</code> up to six. Someone asked what it would take to prove it for all <code>n</code>.</p>
<p>The honest answer is that it would take a different tool. Kani is a bounded model checker, and &quot;bounded&quot; is not a footnote — it is the entire shape of the claim. Six is not infinity. So I went and read the documentation for the three tools people reach for when six isn&#39;t enough.</p>
<p>I&#39;ve shipped work with Kani: 72 harnesses across a Rust statistics library, running as a release gate, with Miri underneath as the undefined-behaviour backstop. I have not <em>verified a system</em> in Lean, Dafny, or Verus — though I have contributed to the Verus compiler, which is a different thing and taught me something specific about how ghost code is erased. Everything below about those three comes from their own docs and papers, and I&#39;ll flag where I&#39;m reasoning rather than reporting.</p>
<h2 id="what-each-one-actually-proves">What each one actually proves</h2>
<p><strong>Kani</strong> reuses the rustc frontend and swaps the backend: instead of lowering MIR to LLVM, it lowers MIR to Goto-C and hands the result to CBMC, which encodes program traces as SAT/SMT problems. The Kani docs describe model checking as &quot;non-random and exhaustive (though often only up to some bound on input or problem size).&quot; That parenthesis is the whole game. What you get is a proof over a bounded input space — genuinely exhaustive within the bound, and silent about everything outside it. In exchange you get panics, arithmetic overflow, out-of-bounds indexing, and several classes of undefined behaviour checked by default, with no specification written at all.</p>
<p><strong>Verus</strong> generates verification conditions from a subset of Rust and discharges them with Z3. No bound. It proves the <code>requires</code>/<code>ensures</code>/<code>invariant</code> clauses you wrote, over all inputs. Its distinguishing idea, from the OOPSLA 2023 paper, is a mode system separating <code>spec</code>, <code>proof</code>, and <code>exec</code> code, where proofs manipulate linear ghost permissions to reason about raw pointers and concurrency. The paper formalises linearity, borrowing, and the modes in a lambda calculus and proves type safety for it — a stronger self-account than most tools in this space offer.</p>
<p><strong>Dafny</strong> is auto-active verification in its own language, powered by Boogie and Z3, compiling out to a general-purpose language afterwards. Same unbounded, specification-relative guarantee as Verus.</p>
<p><strong>Lean</strong> is different in kind. It&#39;s a dependent type theory where a proof is a term, and a small kernel — a few thousand lines of C++ — checks that term against its type. Tactics, <code>mathlib</code>, and every piece of automation sit outside the trusted core and can be arbitrarily buggy without threatening soundness, because the kernel re-checks their output. That&#39;s the de Bruijn criterion, and it&#39;s why a Lean theorem is the strongest artefact of the four. It&#39;s also the reason mathlib can carry over a million lines of formalised mathematics without anyone auditing all of it.</p>
<p>The ordering is real: a Lean theorem &gt; a Verus or Dafny postcondition &gt; a Kani result at bound six. But the ordering runs opposite to effort, and the tools are not substitutes.</p>
<h2 id="where-the-burden-sits">Where the burden sits</h2>
<p>Kani gives you the most for free. <code>cargo kani</code> on an existing crate checks memory safety and overflow before you have written a single specification. The work is harness design — choosing what&#39;s symbolic, what&#39;s assumed, and how big the bound can be before the solver stops terminating.</p>
<p>Dafny and Verus are auto-active: you write invariants and contracts, the SMT solver does the reasoning, and when it fails you get a failed proof obligation rather than an explanation. That&#39;s the characteristic experience and the characteristic frustration. You are debugging a solver&#39;s silence.</p>
<p>Lean inverts it. Automation exists — <code>simp</code>, <code>omega</code>, <code>grind</code> — but the machine is fundamentally a checker, not a searcher. You write the argument.</p>
<h2 id="loops-which-is-where-they-diverge-hardest">Loops, which is where they diverge hardest</h2>
<p>Kani unwinds:</p>
<pre class="shiki shiki-themes github-light github-dark" style="--shiki-light:#24292e;--shiki-dark:#e1e4e8;--shiki-light-bg:#fff;--shiki-dark-bg:#24292e" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="--shiki-light:#24292E;--shiki-dark:#E1E4E8">#[kani</span><span style="--shiki-light:#D73A49;--shiki-dark:#F97583">::</span><span style="--shiki-light:#24292E;--shiki-dark:#E1E4E8">unwind(11)]</span></span></code></pre><p>The bound has to be one more than the actual iteration count. Get it wrong and you get an <em>unwinding assertion</em> failure, and other properties come back undetermined. I want to be fair to Kani here: it tells you. It does not quietly verify a truncated program and report success. There&#39;s also an experimental path — <code>#[kani::loop_invariant(cond)]</code> behind <code>-Z loop-contracts</code>, with optional <code>#[kani::loop_decreases(expr)]</code> — that abstracts loops instead of unrolling them and lifts bounded proofs to unbounded ones. It holds for every natural number. That&#39;s the trade: the most work per property, and no bound anywhere.</p>
<h2 id="the-trust-boundary-which-is-the-actual-decision">The trust boundary, which is the actual decision</h2>
<p>Every one of these has a place where verification stops and trust starts. They differ in where.</p>
<p>Verus verifies the Rust you ship. Ghost code is erased; the executable is compiled by rustc from source you can read. That&#39;s the tightest coupling of the four.</p>
<p>Kani verifies your crate&#39;s MIR, which is close — but the route through Goto-C is a translation you&#39;re trusting, floating-point operations like <code>sqrt</code> are over-approximated, and concurrency isn&#39;t supported at all: Kani warns and compiles concurrent code as if it were sequential. For the numeric-invariant work I do that&#39;s acceptable. For a lock-free queue it would be dangerous, and the warning is easy to scroll past.</p>
<p>Dafny asks you to ship its output. Neither the verifier nor the compilers are proved correct, and soundness bugs have been found in both — that&#39;s stated plainly in the literature motivating recent verified-VCG and verified-compiler work for Dafny. Sources also disagree on backend maturity: the project site lists C#, Java, JavaScript, and Go as solid with Python &quot;in progress,&quot; while release notes and FAQs describe Python as shipped and Rust as in progress or planned. Check the version you&#39;d actually install.</p>
<p>Lean has the smallest trusted kernel and the largest gap to your binary. You either write your program in Lean and trust its (unverified) compiler to C, or you verify a translated model — Aeneas turns safe Rust into a pure functional model in Lean, Rocq, or F*, at the cost of excluding interior mutability and unsafe code — and trust the translation.</p>
<p>There is no option here where nothing is trusted. Pick which unverified thing you&#39;re comfortable with.</p>
<h2 id="a-realistic-first-week">A realistic first week</h2>
<p><strong>Kani</strong>: you get value on day one, before writing any specification, because the default checks find real panics. By day three you&#39;re fighting solver blowup and shrinking bounds. I know this one first-hand — my full suite takes over twenty minutes, which is too slow for a pre-commit hook and fine for a release gate.</p>
<p><strong>Verus</strong>: expect to spend the first day on toolchain setup — it&#39;s not a plain cargo plugin — and much of the week discovering which Rust features are in the supported subset. The project describes itself as under active development, so this moves.</p>
<p><strong>Dafny</strong>: probably the smoothest of the three annotation-based tools. The language was designed around the verifier and you&#39;ll prove a binary search correct fairly quickly. The catch arrives later, when you notice you&#39;re writing Dafny and not writing your product.</p>
<p><strong>Lean</strong>: week one is learning tactics and mathlib naming conventions. You will not verify production code in it. That&#39;s not a failure of the tool; it&#39;s what buying a real theorem costs.</p>
<h2 id="what-id-actually-pick">What I&#39;d actually pick</h2>
<p>If you have a Rust codebase now and no budget for writing specifications: <strong>Kani</strong>. It&#39;s the only one of the four that pays off before you&#39;ve written a spec, and bounded proofs over small <code>n</code> catch essentially all index-arithmetic and boundary bugs. Add Miri, because model checking and interpretation have different blind spots.</p>
<p>If you&#39;re writing Rust where a bound would be a lie — unbounded loops, data-structure invariants, concurrent permissions: <strong>Verus</strong>. It&#39;s the only tool that gives unbounded guarantees about the same source rustc compiles. The price is a moving language subset and a real learning curve.</p>
<p>If you&#39;re designing an algorithm from scratch and will accept shipping generated code: <strong>Dafny</strong>. Best effort-to-guarantee ratio in the middle of the range, and the least friction.</p>
<p>If the property is genuinely mathematical, or the claim has to survive a decade: <strong>Lean</strong>. Nothing else produces an artefact independently checkable by a five-thousand-line kernel.</p>
<p>What I wouldn&#39;t do is reach for Lean to verify an existing Rust codebase. That path exists, but it&#39;s a research toolchain and a restricted subset, and the honest version of that decision is &quot;we&#39;re rewriting for verifiability.&quot;</p>
<p>For my library, the bound at six is still the right call. Not because it&#39;s a strong theorem — it isn&#39;t — but because the properties I care about fail at small <code>n</code> or not at all. The moment I write something where that stops being true, the answer is Verus, and I&#39;d rather know that now than argue it later.</p>
